Farmer Interview Transcription & Translation
Interviewee: Amina Habibu  |  Location: Doka  |  Language: Translated from Hausa to English
1. Introduction & Farm Profile
Respondent: My name is Amina Habibu from Doka.
Interviewer: How many years have you been farming up to now?
Respondent: I have been farming for about 35 years.
Interviewer: What types of crops do you plant on your farm?
Respondent: I plant maize and soybeans, and this year we planted more groundnuts than we usually do. I also plant peppers (bell/chili peppers) and tomatoes.
Interviewer: So you practice rainy season farming?
Respondent: Yes, I do rainy season farming, and we also touch on dry season farming. We don't have access to a dam, so we do it using wells.
Interviewer: What is the size of your farmlands?
Respondent: Well, there is a large farm—what we call a hectare—which spans from one electric pole to the next. There are also smaller plots. In total, I currently use three large farms (hectares) and two smaller plots. Combining the two smaller plots equals about one large plot, making roughly four hectares in total.
2. Intercropping, Strip Cropping & Fertilizer Management
Respondent: On one of the farms, after harvesting and clearing (harrowing) the land, once the rains fall and ridges are made, I plant three ridges of sorghum and three ridges of soybeans. Then I create a boundary line (known locally as kalankuwa) using millet to separate the strips inside the farm.
Respondent: On another farm, you make four ridges of sorghum and three ridges of groundnuts. On yet another, you make four ridges of sorghum and four ridges of soybeans. That is how we plant them.
Interviewer: You plant maize, tomatoes, groundnuts, and sorghum. Do you plant these crops because of market demand, or because that is what you grew up doing based on the local soil and rainfall conditions?
Respondent: For us, farming has become almost like a salaried job with a company. It is our primary capital. It feeds us, gives us water to drink, funds our children's weddings and family events, and allows us to save reserves for the future. When the rainy season ends, you call a buyer; if the produce is of good quality, they inspect it and pay you in a lump sum, and then they take it to process in their factories.
Respondent: As for soybeans, they have a very neat growth habit and don't even require chemical fertilizer—they don't mix well with fertilizer, and as long as you weed and tend to them properly, they do fine on their own. Groundnuts and maize, however, are the crops that require fertilizer, especially maize. For maize, from the time you plant it, you have to apply fertilizer about three times before you harvest it.
3. Crop Rotation & Yield Improvements from University Training
Interviewer: Over the past two to three years, have you noticed changes in your crop yields—did this year surpass last year, or has it stayed steady?
Respondent: About four years ago, we were farming without much modern awareness. Then, by God's grace, the University (Bayero University) came in—we are favored partners of the University here in Doka. Whenever there is a program, they call us, train us, and give us inputs, and I then go out in groups to educate other people as well. Last year's harvest was better than the years before, and this year's harvest doubled last year's. We have benefited immensely from the University's training. Just last month, Professor Amina and Sunusi Gaya came here and held a groundnut field day on one of the farms we planted. We can only thank the University.
Interviewer: Regarding crop rotation—do you plant maize exclusively on one farm this year and then switch that same farm to groundnuts next year, or do you just mix them together?
Respondent: Any farmer who has learned modern farming understands that crop rotation is what brings real yield. If you plant on a farm this year, when the next season comes, you change it and replace it with a different crop; you will see that the yield surpasses what you get when planting the same crop repeatedly. We definitely practice crop rotation. If you plant on a farm this year, next season you rotate to sorghum, pearl millet, or late millet, and then bring the previous crop back later.
4. Adapting to Rainfall Shifts & Short-Duration Seeds
Interviewer: Currently, how do farmers decide what to plant next season? We are developing a software application that uses satellite data, soil analysis, climate change metrics, and rainfall forecasts to recommend the best crops to plant. How are farmers currently managing given recent weather changes?
Respondent: This year, we consulted among ourselves as farmers because the rainy season did not last long—we nearly faced a crisis due to lack of rain. Because of that, we agreed to switch to early-maturing (short-duration) seed varieties rather than long-duration ones. We must rotate our crops and plant early-maturing seeds, because long-duration varieties did not reach full maturity this year—some sorghum and maize didn't fully mature, and tomatoes remained small. Look at the peppers I am sorting right now: normally, a single pepper plant yields a full harvest, but because the rains stopped early, we are harvesting them early and sorting out the damaged ones from the good ones.
Respondent: We realized that as soon as the first rains fall, we must start planting immediately rather than waiting, because of the climate pattern we have entered. Rain came late—we didn't even get rain in August—and the late rains that finally arrived are what saved us, though some sorghum and maize still didn't mature properly.
Interviewer: Given the low rainfall this year, is there a specific crop that would have performed much better during this exact period?
Respondent: Yes, if we had planted cowpeas (beans), they wouldn't have needed as much water as peppers do. We have an early-maturing cowpea variety that matures in 40 days—you harvest it, and it continues producing for another month. If we had known the rains would behave like this, we wouldn't have planted as many peppers.
5. Community Adoption & Satellite Forecasting Application
Interviewer: That is exactly what the app will do: using satellite data and weather forecasts, it can show a chart or alert predicting rainfall for the next 30 days so farmers can make informed decisions.
Respondent: If farmers get access to that, it will be tremendous. Right now, we listen to weather forecasts on the radio advising farmers to plant early-maturing seeds instead of long-duration ones, or to switch from sorghum to cowpeas based on expected rainfall. We have accepted this guidance, and even now, we look up at the sky praying for more rain while seeking practical solutions.
Interviewer: Do you think neighboring farmers and surrounding villages will accept and adopt this technology if introduced?
Respondent: They will definitely accept it. Whenever something beneficial comes along and you try it and see results, you naturally share it with others. People even come from neighboring towns like Gwarzo asking about our crops and how to get the improved seeds, and I direct them to the agricultural extension officer (Malamin Gona) who works closely with us and helps source quality seeds from the University. We are ready and waiting for whatever you bring—we will accept it wholeheartedly.
Interviewer: We would like to visit your farms to take GPS coordinates so that when we begin testing the software, we can use your farms as test cases. Are the farms far from here?
Respondent: We are completely ready. Whenever you come, we will lead you directly to the farms. They aren't far—just a short walk from here.
