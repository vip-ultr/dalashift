1. Introduction &amp; Farm Profile

The participant described themselves as an experienced smallholder in the
validation area. Holding size, tenure and plot sizes are withheld. Both rainy
and dry season farming were reported, with the rainy season taking up more
capacity. Crops reported included sorghum (guinea corn), beans, groundnuts,
white cowpeas and maize.

2. Cropping Systems &amp; Rotation Practices

The participant stated that both rotation and intercropping are practised, with
legumes sown between cereal rows. A crop-level rotation rule was supplied in
their own words: a cereal and legume sequence cycles across plots over a
multi-year period, with a legume or cereal following the previous crop. A
simpler annual swap between two crops was also described. Exact cycle lengths
and plot sizes are withheld as identifying detail.

3. Climate Change Adaptation &amp; Seed Varieties

The change in practice was attributed to agricultural training received through
the university partnership. Traditional varieties were reported as replaced by
modern, shorter-duration ones. Specific maturity day counts are withheld. The
dry-spell strategy was to prepare the land and plant quickly once the soil has
good moisture from the early rains. The participant stressed that planting times
and crop types must be adjusted as the climate changes.

4. Adoption of Satellite Weather Forecasting

The participant said a satellite-based rainfall and weather tool would be
accepted, but that acceptance depends entirely on the approach used to introduce
it. An approach a rural farmer cannot comprehend will not be trusted. They
distinguished immediate adopters from those who wait to see someone else succeed
first.

5. Soil Nutrient &amp; Fertility Management

The account, in order, was to clear and cut back vegetation before planting,
gather and burn it, and apply organic manure. Burning was described as adding
nutrients and reducing disease and pests.

6. Community Engagement &amp; Network Connectivity

The engagement structure described runs from the research sector and
agricultural extension experts through community leaders, village heads,
religious leaders and farmers' association leaders. The participant stressed
that an introducer who does not speak Hausa will struggle to gain acceptance.
Network coverage varies by carrier but was reported as workable, so connectivity
is not the blocker the team assumed.
