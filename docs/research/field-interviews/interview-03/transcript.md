Farmer Interview Transcription & Translation
Interviewee: Abubakar (Dan Hajiya)  |  Location: Doka  |  Language: Translated from Hausa to English
1. Introduction & Farm Profile
Respondent: My name is Abubakar, also known as Dan Hajiya here in Doka.
Respondent: Honestly, my personal farmland is about 30 hectares. This is my own land, not rented. It covers both rainy season farming and dry season farming.
Interviewer: Do you do both?
Respondent: Yes, absolutely, I do both.
Interviewer: Is it 50-50, or do you focus more on one?
Respondent: Well, the rainy season farming takes up more capacity.
Interviewer: What crops do you plant on your farm?
Respondent: During the rainy season, we plant guinea corn (sorghum), beans, groundnuts, white cowpeas, and maize.
Interviewer: What is the size distribution of your fields?
Respondent: Well, there are plots of about three hectares each around here.
2. Cropping Systems & Rotation Practices
Interviewer: Do you practice crop rotation or intercropping?
Respondent: We practice crop rotation so we do both, but mostly—as our farm visits show—if we plant soybeans and sorghum this year, by God's grace, next year the soybean plot changes to sorghum, and the sorghum plot changes to soybeans.
Respondent: There are also parts where we intercrop: we plant sorghum and then sow soybeans in between the sorghum rows, or cowpeas in between the sorghum rows. We do all sorts of variations.
Respondent: However, the rule we follow is: wherever we plant sorghum and soybeans, next time that same spot gets white cowpeas or soybeans, or if it had white cowpeas or soybeans, it gets sorghum or maize, following a two-to-three-year crop rotation cycle.
3. Climate Change Adaptation & Seed Varieties
Interviewer: Have you noticed any changes in your farm's productivity or yields due to climate change?
Respondent: Yes, there have been changes because we attended workshops and training received from Bayero University regarding climate change.
Respondent: This training on climate change forced us to change the crop varieties we work with. Instead of traditional varieties, we shifted to modern ones. For instance, instead of planting sorghum that takes 100 to 120 days to mature, we now plant varieties that mature in 80 to 90 days (about three months), so you finish harvesting early, which is progress.
Respondent: Also, previously when the rains fell, you would plant, but then you might experience dry spells (drought pockets) with no rain. Now, as a result of this training, once the soil gets good moisture from the early rains, we quickly prepare the land and plant. This way, we manage and mitigate dry spells and climate shifts.
Respondent: Now, with these climate and rainfall pattern shifts, the planting seasons and seed types have changed. As I mentioned earlier, planting times and crop types had to be adjusted due to climate change and rainfall variations. If the rainy season shifts by a month, you must also adapt your schedule to find a way forward. If you insist on sticking entirely to traditional farming methods in a changing climate, you will be left behind. So we had to adapt our planting times and crop types.
4. Adoption of Satellite Weather Forecasting
Interviewer: If we introduce an intervention or a solution using satellite technology to forecast rainfall patterns and local weather conditions, would farmers accept it?
Respondent: Yes, they will definitely accept it, but it depends entirely on the approach you use to introduce it. If the approach helps the rural community truly understand what you have brought, they will accept it. But if you come with an approach that a rural farmer cannot comprehend, they won't trust it. So the method of introduction, how the tools are implemented, and how effectively they work in practice will determine whether people accept them. Some people will adopt an innovation immediately when you bring it, while others will wait and adopt it only after seeing someone else succeed with it first. Therefore, the methods you use to introduce and run these tools will determine their level of adoption.
5. Soil Nutrient & Fertility Management
Interviewer: I have another question. May God grant success. Earlier, you talked about rainfall and the crops planted. Now, what I want to know about is soil nutrients. How do soil nutrients affect the crops we grow and the yields we get?
Respondent: Well, regarding soil nutrients, honestly, we usually restore our soil's fertility through clearing and management. The way we manage it is: before planting, we clear bushes, cut down excess growth, gather and burn them, and apply organic manure (what is called takin Hausa or animal manure). This is a way to enrich the soil. Burning organic matter so it settles into the soil adds nutrients and helps reduce diseases and unwanted pests. That is what needs to be done.
6. Community Engagement & Network Connectivity
Interviewer: Regarding network connectivity in these rural areas, sometimes getting a reliable connection can be tricky. How do you deal with that?
Respondent: We think collaborating with extension officers who are close to the communities helps. They understand these local dynamics and act as a bridge between us and the community.
Interviewer: Do you think that approach works?
Respondent: Yes, as I said, the approach you take to introduce your project determines its acceptance. The research sector—like Bayero University—can invite agricultural extension experts to educate them. Then these agricultural experts can engage community leaders, village heads, religious leaders at mosques, and farmers' association leaders. God willing, following this structure will ensure the message reaches where it needs to go and achieves results. But if you just send someone who doesn't speak Hausa to hand out materials and explain things, it will be hard to gain acceptance. You understand? However, if the University trains extension workers who then engage local leaders, that opens a smooth path. That won't be a problem.
Interviewer: Is this the location where you use your data and everything else?
Respondent: Yes, we know this is the most effective method, though dealing with network connectivity can sometimes be tricky. You go to one area and find MTN, and another area you find Airtel. Here, if you don't get MTN, you get Airtel. Even MTN can sometimes fluctuate, but when you come near the main road where the network towers are located, or use setups like this, we manage. So here, if you don't get MTN, you get Airtel. We don't have major network problems; there is no issue.
